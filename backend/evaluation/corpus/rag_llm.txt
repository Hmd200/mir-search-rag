Retrieval-augmented generation sits on top of search rather than replacing it.
A user question is first used to fetch a handful of supporting chunks. Those
chunks are copied into a prompt template and sent to a large language model,
which is instructed to answer only from that material and to mark which pieces
it used. The generation step is what turns ranked passages into a fluent reply.
Without the retrieved context, the model would answer from parameters trained
on the public web, which is exactly how unsupported claims appear.

Hallucination is the name for confident statements that are not licensed by
the evidence. A model can invent a statistic, a citation marker, or even a
document that was never retrieved. Grounding is the countermeasure: the prompt
tells the model to stay inside the supplied chunks, to refuse when the chunks
are insufficient, and to attach citation markers such as [1] that correspond
to numbered sources in the context. After the completion arrives, a validator
can strip markers that point past the last provided chunk so that fabricated
references never reach the user. Citations are not decorative. They are the
audit trail from a sentence in the answer back to a particular passage.

The quality of the generated reply is bounded by the quality of the retrieved
set. If the wrong chunks enter the context window, a perfectly obedient model
will still produce a wrong but well-cited answer. That is why some pipelines
rerank a larger candidate list with a cross-encoder before prompting: a slower
pairwise scorer reorders the top results so that the handful of chunks that
fit in the prompt are the most relevant ones. Query rewriting is another
optional stage, in which the original question is restated before retrieval.
Neither trick replaces the need for a corpus that actually contains the answer.

Abstention matters as much as citation. When nothing in the collection supports
the question, the honest output is to say that the evidence is insufficient
instead of guessing. Systems that always emit a paragraph train users to trust
fluent text. Systems that can stop make the remaining answers easier to check.
In short, retrieval-augmented generation is a contract: search selects the
evidence, the language model composes a reply, and citation plus validation
keep that reply tied to real passages. The method fails when retrieval misses,
when the prompt is too weak to prevent invention, or when citation markers are
treated as optional garnish rather than as required provenance.

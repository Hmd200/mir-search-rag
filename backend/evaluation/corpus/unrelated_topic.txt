Raised-bed vegetable growing rewards attention to soil more than to gadgets.
A workable bed is about eight inches deep, filled with a mix of finished
compost, screened topsoil, and a little coarse sand so that water drains
instead of pooling around roots. Fresh kitchen scraps do not belong in the
bed itself; they belong in a compost pile where heat and air can break them
down. Turn the pile weekly, keep it as damp as a wrung-out sponge, and wait
until the material is dark, crumbly, and no longer smells sour before you
spread it. Unfinished compost steals nitrogen from seedlings and can burn
tender stems.

Mulch is the quiet labor-saver. A two-inch blanket of shredded leaves or
straw shades the surface, slows evaporation, and keeps rain from packing the
soil into a crust. Pull the mulch back a few inches from each stem so that
collars stay dry and slugs have fewer hiding places. Tomatoes want a stake or
cage before they sprawl. Once the plant is knee-high, pinch the suckers that
sprout in the leaf axils if you are growing indeterminate varieties; that
channels energy into fruit instead of a tangle of vines. Determinate bush
types need less pinching. Water at the soil line in the morning so foliage
dries before night, which reduces blight.

Harvest timing is a skill of looking, not of calendars. Basil is best cut
just as flower spikes begin to form. Take stems down to a pair of strong
side shoots so the plant branches instead of sulking. Bundle the cuttings
and hang them in a dark, airy cupboard until the leaves crumble, then store
the crumble in a jar away from sunlight. Cherry tomatoes come off when they
are fully colored and smell sweet at the stem scar; they continue to soften
on a counter but they will not gain sugar once picked green. Beans are
tender when the pod still snaps. Leave them much longer and the seeds bulge,
the pod turns leathery, and the plant slows production.

Winter prep is mostly cleanup. Pull spent vines, add a last layer of compost,
and cover empty beds with mulch or a living cover such as clover so that
bare earth does not crust or wash away. Clean cages with a brush, and let
wooden sides dry before stacking them. Label seed packets with the year they
were packed, and keep them in a tin in a cool cupboard. Next spring the same
beds will already hold structure, organic matter, and a head start on weeds
you never let set seed. It is dirt, water, sunlight, and patience: the slow
work of growing food rather than of writing about anything else.
